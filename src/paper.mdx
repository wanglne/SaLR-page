<section id="abstract" aria-labelledby="abstract-title">

<h2 id="abstract-title">Abstract</h2>

Latent space reasoning improves inference efficiency by compressing chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning into continuous latent states, but its safety alignment remains poorly understood. In this work, we conduct the first systematic safety study of latent space reasoning models under jailbreak attacks. Our results reveal that most latent reasoning methods exhibit higher vulnerability to jailbreak attacks than both the base model and token-space CoT reasoning counterparts, suggesting that moving reasoning into latent states can weaken safety alignment. To address this issue, we propose **SaLR**, a **S**afety-**a**ware **L**atent space **R**easoning framework that injects safety supervision into latent space reasoning. SaLR converts long-form safety reasoning into a compact four-block safe-chain and transfers this safety signal through teacher-student hidden state distillation. For safety instances, SaLR aligns an early safe response prefix to guide the model toward a safe response trajectory; for reasoning instances, it retains single-position distillation to maintain reasoning utility. Experiments across model scales and harmful benchmarks show that SaLR substantially reduces jailbreak attack success while retaining most of the reasoning utility and token efficiency. Beyond standard jailbreaks, SaLR also shows robustness to reasoning-specific attacks such as token inflation and CoT hijacking, while using a fixed latent reasoning budget and avoiding exposed textual reasoning traces. Overall, SaLR provides a favorable safety–utility trade-off for latent space reasoning. Code is available in the [SaLR repository](https://github.com/wanglne/SaLR).

</section>

<section id="overview" aria-labelledby="overview-title">

<h2 id="overview-title">Overview</h2>

### A safety blind spot in efficient reasoning

Latent reasoning replaces long, visible chains of thought with a small number of continuous hidden states. This makes reasoning more compact, but it also changes where safety must be learned—and how it can be evaluated.

Our evaluation reveals a recurring problem: **existing latent reasoning baselines are generally more vulnerable to jailbreaks than the base model and explicit chain-of-thought counterparts.** On 1B AdvBench, for example, CODI reaches 90.9% attack success under GCG, compared with 38.8% for the base model. SaLR brings this down to 2.7%.

<section id="methodology" aria-labelledby="methodology-title">

<h2 id="methodology-title">Methodology</h2>

### Safety belongs where the answer begins

SaLR treats safety as part of learning to reason in latent space. A compact safety trace preserves four decisions: **understand the request, assess the risk, choose an action, and provide a safe continuation.** This gives the model a concise safety signal that fits the limited capacity of latent reasoning.

During training, a teacher transfers this signal through hidden states at the beginning of the safe response. Mathematical examples retain their reasoning-focused supervision. At inference, the student reasons with a fixed latent budget, without generating a long, exposed chain of thought.

<PaperFigure name="method" alt="SaLR teacher–student training: compact safe-chains and mathematical reasoning traces supervise a latent student. Safety examples align a four-token response prefix, while math examples align a single position at answer onset.">
  <strong>Two kinds of supervision, one compact reasoner.</strong> Safety examples use a four-token response prefix for hidden-state distillation; math examples use a single position at answer onset. The evaluated latent reasoning budget is six steps.
</PaperFigure>

For the full training objective, experimental setup, ablations, and additional results, see the [paper](https://wanglne.github.io/papers/SaLR_NIPS2026.pdf). Training and evaluation code is available in the [SaLR repository](https://github.com/wanglne/SaLR).

<section id="results" aria-labelledby="results-title">

<h2 id="results-title">Results</h2>

### A better safety–utility balance

SaLR keeps reported jailbreak success at or below 3.1% across the evaluated 1B, 3B, and 8B settings. Its benefit goes beyond rejecting harmful requests: it preserves much of the reasoning ability of latent models and keeps their compact generation budget.

<Results />

### Safety gains without blanket refusal

On XSTest and OKTest, SaLR maintains strong refusal of harmful requests while matching or improving benign over-refusal rates relative to the evaluated base and safety-augmented models. For the 3B model, unsafe refusal is **95.5%**, while benign over-refusal is **2.0% on XSTest** and **0.33% on OKTest**.

### The way safety is taught matters

Simply adding the original SafeChain data to CODI lowers jailbreak success but substantially reduces reasoning accuracy. On 1B OOD math tasks, CODI + SafeChain reaches **42.62%** average accuracy; SaLR reaches **55.74%** with the same average token count. Compact safety supervision makes a meaningful difference to what the model retains.

The trade-off is still real: SaLR’s in-domain GSM8K-Aug accuracy is 2.33–3.12 percentage points below CODI across the three scales. OOD accuracy is nearly unchanged at 1B and 3B, with a 1.71-point drop at 8B. The result is a favorable balance of safety, utility, and efficiency.

### What if the attack targets reasoning itself?

An attacker may try to make a model think for much longer, or hijack its chain of thought. These attacks expose another reason to study the reasoning process itself: a safe final answer is only one part of reliable behavior.
