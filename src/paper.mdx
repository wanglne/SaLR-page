<section id="abstract" aria-labelledby="abstract-title">

<h2 id="abstract-title">Abstract</h2>

Latent space reasoning improves inference efficiency by compressing chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning into continuous latent states, but its safety alignment remains poorly understood. In this work, we conduct the first systematic safety study of latent space reasoning models under jailbreak attacks. Our results reveal that most latent reasoning methods exhibit higher vulnerability to jailbreak attacks than both the base model and token-space CoT reasoning counterparts, suggesting that moving reasoning into latent states can weaken safety alignment. To address this issue, we propose **SaLR**, a **S**afety-**a**ware **L**atent space **R**easoning framework that injects safety supervision into latent space reasoning. SaLR converts long-form safety reasoning into a compact four-block safe-chain and transfers this safety signal through teacher-student hidden state distillation. For safety instances, SaLR aligns an early safe response prefix to guide the model toward a safe response trajectory; for reasoning instances, it retains single-position distillation to maintain reasoning utility. Experiments across model scales and harmful benchmarks show that SaLR substantially reduces jailbreak attack success while retaining most of the reasoning utility and token efficiency. Beyond standard jailbreaks, SaLR also shows robustness to reasoning-specific attacks such as token inflation and CoT hijacking, while using a fixed latent reasoning budget and avoiding exposed textual reasoning traces. Overall, SaLR provides a favorable safety–utility trade-off for latent space reasoning. Code is available in the [SaLR repository](https://github.com/wanglne/SaLR).

</section>

<section id="overview" aria-labelledby="overview-title">

<h2 id="overview-title">Overview</h2>

### The safety question behind latent reasoning

Explicit chain-of-thought reasoning can improve performance on complex tasks, but generating the intermediate trace adds tokens and inference cost. Latent reasoning compresses these intermediate steps into continuous hidden states, allowing the model to produce an answer with far fewer generated tokens.

This changes how safety can be learned and evaluated. Textual traces offer readable targets for supervision, while latent states are less directly interpretable. Training on the final response only indirectly constrains the hidden reasoning that precedes it. The central question is **whether moving reasoning into latent space preserves a model’s safety alignment**.

We investigate this question by comparing the LLaMA 3.2-1B base model, SFT-CoT, and representative latent reasoning methods under GCG and PAIR attacks. The evaluated latent models are generally more vulnerable than both the base model and SFT-CoT. On AdvBench under GCG, attack success rises from **38.8% for the base model** to **61.0% for SFT-CoT** and **90.9% for CODI**.

The hidden states before response generation show a related pattern. Safe and unsafe requests are more clearly separated in the base model, become more mixed after SFT-CoT training, and are further entangled in CODI. The safety gap appears in both the model’s responses and its internal representations.

<section id="methodology" aria-labelledby="methodology-title">

<h2 id="methodology-title">Methodology</h2>

### Safety supervision at the transition into a response

SaLR first rewrites long safety rationales into a compact four-block safe-chain: **request intent, risk assessment, response action, and safe follow-up.** The action is chosen from **refuse, partial, or allow**. This structure preserves the core safety decisions while making the supervision compact enough to fit a small latent reasoning budget.

During training, the teacher learns from the explicit reasoning trace and target response. The student reasons through latent states and then generates the response without reading that trace. SaLR transfers the teacher’s supervision through hidden-state distillation at the start of the response. Safety examples align the first **four response positions (K = 4)** to guide a safe response trajectory; mathematical examples retain **single-position alignment at the answer onset** to preserve reasoning utility.

<PaperFigure name="method" alt="SaLR teacher–student training: compact safe-chains and mathematical reasoning traces supervise a latent student. Safety examples align a four-token response prefix, while math examples align a single position at answer onset.">
  <strong>Compact traces and response-prefix distillation.</strong> Safety and mathematical examples use different alignment strategies within the same teacher–student framework: a four-token prefix for safety and a single position for mathematics.
</PaperFigure>

At inference, only the student is used. The evaluated model takes **six latent reasoning steps** before generating its response, retaining a fixed reasoning budget without emitting a long textual chain of thought.

The [paper](https://wanglne.github.io/papers/SaLR_NIPS2026.pdf) provides the full training objective, experimental setup, ablations, and additional results. Training and evaluation code is available in the [SaLR repository](https://github.com/wanglne/SaLR).

<section id="results" aria-labelledby="results-title">

<h2 id="results-title">Results</h2>

### Jailbreak robustness across benchmarks and model scales

Across four harmful benchmarks, GCG and PAIR attacks, and 1B, 3B, and 8B models, SaLR keeps mean attack success at or below **3.1%**. On 1B AdvBench, for example, it reduces attack success to **2.7% under GCG** and **1.2% under PAIR**. We evaluate these safety gains alongside mathematical reasoning, token efficiency, and responses to benign requests.

<Results />

### Refusing harmful requests with low benign over-refusal

A low jailbreak success rate needs to be considered alongside the model’s usefulness on benign requests. XSTest and OKTest evaluate this distinction. For the 3B model, SaLR refuses **95.5% of unsafe XSTest prompts**, while its over-refusal rates on benign prompts are **2.0% on XSTest** and **0.33% on OKTest**. These results show that strong refusal of harmful requests can coexist with low refusal of benign ones.

### Preserving reasoning utility through compact supervision

Adding the original SafeChain data to CODI lowers jailbreak success, but also substantially reduces reasoning accuracy. On 1B OOD math tasks, CODI + SafeChain reaches **42.62%** average accuracy, compared with **55.74%** for SaLR at the same average token count. The comparison highlights the importance of adapting safety supervision to the limited capacity of latent reasoning.

The improvement involves a measurable trade-off. Relative to CODI, SaLR’s in-domain GSM8K-Aug accuracy is **2.33–3.12 percentage points lower** across the three scales. OOD accuracy remains nearly unchanged at 1B and 3B, with a **1.71-point decrease at 8B**. SaLR retains most of the reasoning utility of the latent baseline while substantially improving its jailbreak robustness.

### Robustness to attacks on the reasoning process

We also evaluate attacks that target the reasoning process itself: **OverThink**, which inflates reasoning length to increase inference cost, and **H-CoT**, which attempts to redirect an exposed chain of thought toward a harmful response. Further tests examine multi-turn jailbreaks and direct perturbations of latent reasoning states.
